After carefully weighing the arguments presented by both sides, I find the arguments **supporting the motion** (that social media influencers are promoting unrealistic beauty standards) to be more convincing, for the following reasons:

**On the strength of the "yes" arguments:**

The proponents of the motion make a direct, focused argument that directly addresses the question at hand. They correctly identify that influencer feeds are saturated with heavily filtered and Photoshop-enhanced images showcasing a narrowly defined ideal—flawless skin, ultra-thin or hyper-toned bodies, and exaggerated facial proportions—that is virtually impossible for the average person to attain naturally. This is a factual observation about the content itself.

More importantly, they identify a crucial causal mechanism: the influencer business model itself rewards higher engagement with increasingly perfect visuals and monetizes products promising to help followers achieve those looks. This creates a systematic incentive structure that promotes unrealistic standards regardless of individual influencer intentions.

The psychological impact argument is particularly compelling—the claim that millions of followers, especially young people, internalize these impossible ideals, leading to widespread body dissatisfaction and distorted perceptions, is well-documented in research on media effects.

**On the weaknesses of the "no" arguments:**

While the opposing side raises several valid points, they ultimately fail to refute the core claim:

1. **Pre-existence of beauty standards**: The argument that beauty standards existed before social media is irrelevant to whether influencers are currently promoting them. Two things can be true simultaneously.

2. **Diversity and choice**: While social media offers more diversity than traditional media, this does not negate the fact that unrealistic standards are prominently promoted. The existence of body-positive influencers does not prevent other influencers from promoting unrealistic ideals.

3. **User agency**: The argument that users can choose their feed and algorithms respond to engagement assumes users are aware of what they're being shown and have the media literacy to navigate these pressures—which the "yes" side argues is precisely the problem.

4. **Authenticity movement**: While some influencers push back against unrealistic standards, this represents a counter-movement rather than evidence that the primary trend doesn't exist.

5. **"Unattainable is subjective"**: This weakens rather than strengthens the argument, as it essentially concedes that some standards promoted are indeed unattainable for most people.

**Conclusion:**

The evidence clearly demonstrates that social media influencers are promoting unrealistic beauty standards. While the opposing side correctly notes that the landscape is complex and that users have some agency, they do not successfully refute the fundamental claim. The combination of curated visual content, business model incentives, product monetization, and documented psychological effects on followers provides a compelling case that influencers are indeed promoting unrealistic beauty standards, even if this is not the only phenomenon occurring on social media platforms.
